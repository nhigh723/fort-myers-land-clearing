Brush Clearing & Bush Hogging in Fort Myers, FL

Brush clearing is the cheapest way to take back a lot that's grown up in Fort Myers. A bush hog cuts tall grass, palmetto, and saplings down to the ground in a single pass — no trucks, no hauling, and usually done the same day for a typical residential lot.

What brush clearing handles

Brush clearing sits at the light end of land clearing. The tool is a rotary cutter — a bush hog — mounted on a tractor or skid steer. It powers through growth that a mower can't touch:

  • Overgrown grass and weeds — the waist-high growth that takes over a vacant lot after a few months of rain.
  • Saw palmetto — the low, spiky fans that carpet Southwest Florida lots and laugh at lawnmowers.
  • Young saplings and thin woody stems — small trees and brush up to roughly an inch or two thick.
  • Vines and tangled scrub — the knotted growth that fills the gaps between everything else.

Everything cut stays where it falls and breaks down on the ground. That is what keeps the job quick and inexpensive — there is no second machine, no loading, and no haul-off. A lot that has sat for a season or two usually comes back to visible, walkable, and maintainable with one pass.

Where brush clearing stops

A bush hog is a light tool, and it has real limits. It will not handle:

  • Anything with a real trunk — established trees need a saw crew or a forestry mulcher, not a mower.
  • Dense pepper or melaleuca — thick stands of Brazilian pepper or melaleuca are mulcher work. A bush hog bounces off them or breaks down trying.
  • Stumps — cutting works above ground level. Stumps stay exactly where they are unless you add stump grinding.
  • Anything that isn't vegetation — fencing, concrete, dumped junk, and storm debris have to be picked up and hauled by hand or machine.

A quick way to tell which one you need

Walk the lot. If you can push through the growth — even if it takes effort — brush clearing will probably do it. If the pepper and palmetto form a wall you can't get through, you're past bush hog territory and into forestry mulching. A few photos are usually enough for a local pro to tell you which one fits; send them when you request a quote.

When brush clearing makes sense

  • Maintaining a vacant lot — overgrown parcels can draw code enforcement complaints in the city and county, and a seasonal cut is the cheapest way to stay on the right side of that — and to keep the lot visible to neighbors and buyers.
  • Seeing the lot for the first time — before buying, selling, or building, one cut shows you the lot's shape, its low spots, and whatever has been hiding in the growth.
  • A first pass before the heavy work — cutting the light growth first exposes stumps, debris, and access routes, so a mulching or clearing crew can price the real job accurately instead of guessing.
  • Fire and pest control — tall, dry growth next to a house is a fire risk and a hiding place for pests. A seasonal cut keeps it down, and it matters most on lots that sit between built homes in south Fort Myers neighborhoods.

What it costs

Brush clearing is normally the lowest-priced clearing option — quoted by the acre or by the hour, and priced below mulching and full clearing because the equipment is lighter and nothing gets hauled. What you actually pay depends on how tall and dense the growth is, the size of the lot, and how easily a machine can get onto it. In-town lots with tight access can cost more per acre than open parcels, even though they're smaller.

The range is wide enough that a real quote is the only honest answer: most Fort Myers brush jobs get priced after a look at the lot or a few photos, and a local pro can usually give you a number the same day. See how brush clearing stacks up against the other options in the cost guide.

How often to cut

Southwest Florida grows fast. A lot that looks tidy in March can be overgrown by July. Owners who keep vacant lots maintained usually cut twice a year — once before the wet season starts (around May) and once after it ends (October or November). Lots with aggressive pepper may need a third pass. Staying on that schedule keeps the growth light enough that you never need the expensive equipment at all.

Questions to ask before you hire

  • Is the price by the acre, by the hour, or for the whole lot?
  • Is my lot's growth light enough for a bush hog, or do I need a mulcher?
  • Does the cut material stay on the ground, and is that fine for what I have planned?
  • Are there stumps, wet spots, or debris on the lot that would change the price?
  • Can you cut on a recurring schedule, and is the per-visit price lower?
